Research Program

Turning materials design from search into derivation

Rather than screening candidates and testing them, engineers should be able to reason from a target mechanical response back to the molecular structure and processing conditions that produce it, guided by the physical principles involved. My research resolves the assembled, chemically crosslinked, and physically entangled nanostructure of polymeric and nanocomposite materials, traces how it governs mechanical response, and turns network topology from a hidden consequence of synthesis into a design variable.

The Foundation: Topology Controls Mechanics

My dissertation established how network topology in polymer nanocomposites and soft polymeric networks controls emergent mechanical behavior, across three very different material classes.

Polymer-grafted nanoparticles

Coarse-grained MD and theory showed how interdigitation and entanglement modulate nanoconfinement, governing stiffness, toughness, and gas transport. These insights guide lightweight, high-strength materials and tunable separation membranes.

Crosslinked polymer networks

Graph-theoretic representations link heterogeneous connectivity, including loops, dangling chains, and entanglements, to large-deformation mechanics and fracture.

Micellar hydrogels

Long molecular bridges between micelles toughen the gel and enable cooling-induced shape memory, confirmed by experimental collaborators in synthesized gels. Network connectivity is a design lever for soft robotics and biomedical devices.

Each insight required costly simulation of individual networks. To make topology a practical design variable, I built TANGO.

Research Highlight

TANGO

Topology-Aware Neural Graph Operator for material constitutive laws
ICLR 2026 • AI4MATSCI Workshop ICLR Travel Grant Full paper in preparation

A four-minute explainer: why topology matters, how TANGO reads a network, and how it designs one.

The problem. Two polymer networks built from the same ingredients can respond completely differently, because what matters is how the chains are wired: crosslinks, entanglements, loops, and dangling ends. Resolving that with molecular simulation costs hours of supercomputer time per network, which makes searching over network architectures intractable.

The approach. TANGO reads a network as a graph and uses edge-conditioned graph attention to learn a topology embedding. Queried at any strain, it predicts the full nonlinear stress–strain response through fracture, with calibrated aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty. Trained on coarse-grained MD, it matches simulation accuracy at more than seven orders of magnitude lower cost, fast enough for inverse design: specify a target curve, and retrieve the network that produces it.

Research Agenda

Three Efforts Around the Loop

Full derivation is a long-term goal. I approach it in tractable stages, and the efforts are deliberately decoupled so that each produces standalone results without depending on the others.

1Closing the process–structure–mechanics loop

Processing → Molecular network

This effort extends design upstream, from specifying structure to controlling the processes that create it. I will pursue it in self-assembled Pluronic hydrogels, where composition, concentration, and temperature govern micellar networks whose size, packing, and connectivity set macroscopic mechanics. My preliminary studies show that synthesis conditions generate distinct populations of network structures, suggesting that graph generative models trained on coarse-grained MD can learn the mapping from processing to emergent structure.

Pluronics are inexpensive, thermoreversible, and characterizable with standard rheology and scattering, so closed-loop validation is feasible with a single experimental partner. They are also used in injectable and printable biomaterials, extracellular-matrix mimics, and biofabricated scaffolds, which makes synthesis conditions a deliberate design parameter with real applications.

Builds on: micellar-hydrogel connectivity, toughness, and shape memory (Adv. Funct. Mater. 2026) • ongoing collaboration with the Truby group • TANGO as the structure→mechanics link.

2Extracting interpretable laws from learned mechanics

Molecular network → Mechanics, made inspectable

A result no one can inspect cannot be certified for safety-critical design. TANGO predicts how complex topologies produce mechanical responses, but not why particular structural features control them. This effort moves beyond prediction to discover the physical principles organizing the structure–property map.

TANGO serves as a mechanics surrogate that generates large-scale structure–response data. Symbolic regression, informed by established physical principles, proposes candidate laws, which are constrained by dimensional consistency, frame indifference, and thermodynamic validity, then falsified with targeted simulations. Iterative refinement turns empirical relationships into mechanistic laws. The same approach audits classical elasticity and fracture scaling relations, identifying where they hold, where heterogeneous networks break them, and which structural variables extend them.

Interpretable laws also let this work outlive its software: an engineer can learn, test, and apply a scaling relation without ever running the underlying model.

Builds on: TANGO's physically organized embedding space • graph descriptors of hydrogel topology and fracture • CAMEL's causal process–structure–property knowledge graphs from the literature.

3Discovering materials with novel mechanical behavior

Target mechanics → Network → Material

This effort targets property combinations often treated as fundamentally opposed: stiffness with toughness, strength with extensibility, and performance with reprocessability. It turns the framework into a discovery engine that derives molecular network structure from the function a material must perform. I have already used TANGO as a surrogate for direct CGMD to identify networks that match prescribed stress–strain responses, with selected designs validated in simulation.

Next, generative graph models will propose physically realizable networks for target responses, and calibrated uncertainty will route unreliable predictions to higher-fidelity validation, so that every design stays linked to the structural features that govern it. A common network representation will extend this across micellar and colloidal gels, polymer–nanoparticle composites, bio-inspired protein networks, and vitrimers with load-responsive bonds, revealing which mechanical relationships are general and which are system-specific.

Builds on: TANGO inverse design • PGN, hydrogel, and resilin-like protein networks as test beds across material classes.

Long-Term Vision
My long-term goal is to let engineers derive molecular network structures from a desired material behavior, with the physical principles governing a material kept visible throughout design rather than hidden inside a model.

The central challenge is an invertible description of how complex networks give rise to behavior, connected in an unbroken chain from the processing conditions engineers control, through the structures those conditions produce, to the response that emerges. Over the next decade I aim to make this a transferable approach to materials design that unites mechanics, molecular simulation, and machine learning, with every link computationally tractable and physically interpretable.

Because these methods are computational and interpretable, they lower the cost of materials discovery and put it within reach of groups without large experimental or simulation infrastructure. I want to build tools other researchers reach for, aimed at the materials society most needs to improve, from recyclable polymers to durable biomedical and lightweight structural materials.
