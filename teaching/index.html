Teaching & Mentorship

Developing independent thinkers

Teaching Goal
My goal is to develop independent thinkers who reason from fundamental principles rather than rely on learned procedures.

That independence takes judgment: knowing which physical principles govern a problem, when a result can be trusted, and what ethical responsibilities follow from acting on it. I cultivate that judgment by building physical intuition through active exploration and by guiding students toward independent verification. I have developed this approach by teaching structural dynamics, contributing to graduate course development in computational nanomechanics, and mentoring undergraduate and junior graduate researchers.

Teaching Philosophy

Intuition through active exploration

Build intuition before formalism

Students develop stronger conceptual understanding when they build intuition about a problem before formalizing it mathematically. As a teaching assistant for Structural Dynamics, I began recitations with qualitative reasoning and predictions before introducing derivations. I asked students to sketch mode shapes, anticipate limiting behavior, and interpret intermediate results. In problem sessions and in several full lectures I taught, students were more engaged when I paced problems around key ideas, paused to connect each step to the underlying concept, and invited them to explain their reasoning.

We used Google Colab demonstrations in which students completed portions of code, varied the parameters of a multi-degree-of-freedom building, and visualized its response, so they could test their predictions and connect equations to physical behavior. I plan to pair such activities with small-group discussion, so that students develop and compare their reasoning before the class examines different approaches together. Formal tools then deepen understanding and give students a framework they can carry across problems, methods, and disciplines.

Engineers who audit, not only produce

Know when a result can be trusted

Students who understand what a solution should mean are better equipped to recognize an unreasonable result, question its assumptions, and decide whether it can be trusted. This matters more as generative AI and other computational tools produce code, analyses, and designs with little effort. I designed a course project in which students used a large language model to produce a first-pass design and analysis of a structure's earthquake response, then audited it:

GenerateDraft the design and analysis with an LLM
Check assumptionsAre the model and loading physically right?
VerifyUnit consistency and numerical stability
CompareAgainst an independent solution
DefendDocument failures, revise, and justify the final answer

Traditional problem sets remain valuable, but they should not ignore the tools students will meet beyond the classroom. I design assignments that embrace those tools while shifting the focus from producing an answer to understanding, testing, and defending it. AI can accelerate coding, optimization, and design exploration, but using it well requires knowing when its output can be trusted, and that distinction is essential to responsible engineering practice.

Engineering with judgment and responsibility

How we engineer matters as much as what we can engineer

At my core, I am a civil engineer, and I see engineering as a way to improve society through the built environment and the materials that enable it. Although my research spans materials science, nanomechanics, chemistry, and computation, that broader purpose should stay central to engineering education. Ethical judgment belongs throughout the curriculum, not only in design courses: students should weigh the consequences of the materials they develop, the assumptions behind the models they use, and the tools they trust.

I bring these questions into technical assignments through case studies and discussion, from the responsible use of AI to the environmental consequences of emerging materials, and I work to make these conversations a safe space where students raise concerns, challenge assumptions, and consider perspectives different from their own.

Mentoring & Advising

Building independence

I view mentorship as building independence, in research and in the classroom. Through Northwestern's REU program (2023–2025), I mentored two undergraduate researchers from their initial questions through analysis, technical reports, and presentations in polymer mechanics and computational modeling.

Less experienced researchers benefit from clear structure early, including a well-defined question and milestones, followed by growing ownership as they gain confidence. As a faculty mentor I will extend this to graduate researchers within a group culture grounded in inclusive collaboration, ethical research, open code, and continual reflection.

Aspiring Future Faculty Program

In 2023 I founded the Aspiring Future Faculty Program in Northwestern CEE, a seminar series connecting students interested in academic careers with faculty at different career stages and with members of grant-writing and review committees. Workshops cover research statements, teaching philosophies, and the faculty job market.

It gives students access to conversations about academic careers that are usually learned informally. Supporting the next generation of engineers extends beyond my own classroom and research group.
